▪ Theory: Synchronisation is a very common problem which arises when multiple 
entities are competing for access to shared resources; 
▪ Theory: In a distributed / parallel system the synchronisation problem is 
complicated by the need to allow for transmission delays i.e. latency in passing 
messages through the IPC between tasks (processes), which makes some 
solutions used in operating systems problematic – latency matters!
▪ Practical skills: Data dependency problems at a process (program) level 
impose precedence relationships that are a type of synchronization problem that 
must be resolved for bug-free code operation.
▪ Practical skills: Failure to properly address synchronisation problems will result 
in often catastrophic application failures or bugs, often very difficult to isolate 
and correct.

What is Synchronisation?

=== PAGE 5 ===
5
▪ A Distributed System consists of a collection of distinct processes that are spatially 
separated and run concurrently;
▪ In systems with multiple concurrent processes, it is economical to share the system 
resources;
▪ Sharing may be cooperative or competitive;
▪ Both competitive and cooperative sharing require adherence to certain rules of behavior 
that guarantee that correct interaction occurs – otherwise chaotic behaviour may arise;
▪ The rules of enforcing correct interaction are implemented in the form of synchronization 
mechanisms;
▪ Synchronisation mechanisms may be part of an operating system, or communications 
mechanism like a library;
▪ Even if the synchronization mechanism is embedded in a library and “invisible” it is prudent 
to know what mechanism is used and how it behaves in case a failure does arise
Synchronisation in Parallel / Distributed Systems

=== PAGE 6 ===
6
▪ In single CPU systems, synchronization problems such as mutual exclusion 
can be solved using semaphores and monitors. These methods rely on the 
existence of shared memory, which can be accessed very quickly by all 
tasks;
▪ We cannot use semaphores and monitors in distributed systems since two 
processes running on different machines cannot expect to have access to 
any shared memory;
▪ In a distributed system there are always finite time delays – i.e. latency - for 
messages to travel from one process to another, so any mechanism we use 
must account for these delays;
▪ Even simple matters such as determining if one event happened before 
another event require careful thought.
Issues in Implementing Synchronization

=== PAGE 7 ===
7
▪ In distributed systems, it is usually not possible and often not desirable to 
collect all the information about the system in one place and synchronization 
among processes is difficult due to the following features of distributed 
systems:
a) The relevant information is scattered among multiple machines.
b) Processes make decisions based only on local information.
c) Any single point of failure in the system should be avoided.
d) No common clock or other precise global time source exists (the GPS master 
clock may not be available).
▪ Synchronisation in distributed / parallel systems requires unique algorithms 
which account for the unique behaviours in such systems, especially time 
delays.

Why Should We Care About Time in Distributed Systems?

=== PAGE 10 ===
10
▪ Time is unambiguous in a centralized system – every process “sees” the same master 
clock in the machine.
▪ A process can simply make a system call to learn the time – the operating system then 
looks at the clock hardware.
▪ If process A asks for the current time, and a little later process B asks for the time, the 
value of B_time > A_time.
▪ In a distributed system, if process A and B are on different machines, B_time may not be 
greater than A_time.
▪ This is because the hardware clocks on these machines may not be precisely 
synchronised.
▪ Even if the clocks are synchronised, there may be a synchronisation error which is large 
enough to matter.
▪ Example: Recent CERN quantum physics experiment failure.

Cristian’s Algorithm

=== PAGE 18 ===
18
▪ Unlike Cristian’s Algorithm the server process in Berkeley algorithm, called the master 
periodically polls other slave process. 
▪ Generally speaking the algorithm is as follows:
a) A master is chosen with a ring based election algorithm (e.g. Chang and Roberts algorithm).
b) The master polls the slaves who reply with their time in a similar way to Cristian's algorithm
c) The master observes the round -trip time (RTT) of the messages and estimates the time of 
each slave and its own. 
d) The master then averages the clock times, ignoring any values it receives far outside the 
values of the others (outliers suggest a broken clock rather than a drifting clock). 
e) Instead of sending the updated current time back to the other process, the master then 
sends out the amount (positive or negative) that each slave must adjust its clock. This 
avoids further uncertainty due to RTT at the slave processes. 
f) Everybody adjusts their time.
The Berkeley Algorithm (Gusella and Zatti)

=== PAGE 19 ===
19
▪ Both Cristian ’s algorithm and the Berkeley algorithm are centralized 
algorithms with the disadvantages such as the existence of the single 
point of failure (master/server clock process) and high traffic volume 
concentrated in the master clock server.
▪ The “Averaging algorithm” is a decentralized algorithm.
a) This algorithm divides time into resynchronization intervals with a fixed 
length R.
b) Every machine broadcasts the current time at the beginning of each 
interval according to its clock.
c) A machine collects all other broadcasts for a certain interval and sets 
the local clock by using the average of the arrival times.

▪ Lamport showed that:
o Clock synchronization need not be absolute
o If two processes do not interact their clocks need not be synchronized.
o What matters is they agree on the order in which events occur.
▪ For many purposes, it is sufficient that all interacting machines agree on the 
same time – they share a “frame of reference”. It is not essential that this 
agreed time is the same as “real time”. 
a) Clocks which agree across a group of computers but not necessarily with “real 
time” are termed “logical clocks”.
b) Clocks that agree in time values, within a certain time limit (i.e. error), are 
“physical clocks”.

Example: RFC958-5905 Network Time Protocol

=== PAGE 27 ===
27
▪ Originally designed by David L. Mills and published as IETF RFC958 Network 
Time Protocol (NTP) – NTPv4 in RFC5905
▪ Employs a hierarchical tree of time servers that derive their time from multiple 
reference sources to get best possible estimates
▪ Servers arranged in four “strata” where Stratum 0 comprises reference sources 
like atomic clocks and master clocks in navigation satellite systems
▪ Computers in Strata 1, 2 and 3 derive their time from higher strata, and may 
query peers to “sanity check” their time estimates
▪ There may be up to 15 strata in a network with increasing error levels with 
increasing stratum level
▪ Some proprietary schemes derive time from NTP servers
Example: RFC958-5905 Network Time Protocol

=== PAGE 28 ===

Network Time Protocol (NTP) – Mills 1985


=== PAGE 29 ===
29
▪ “The destination peer calculates the roundtrip delay and clock offset relative to 
the source peer as follows.  
▪ Let t1, t2  and t3 represent the contents of the Originate Timestamp, Receive 
Timestamp and Transmit Timestamp fields and t4 the local time the NTP 
message is received.  Then the roundtrip delay d and clock offset c is:
▪          d = (t4 - t1) - (t3 - t2)  and  c = (t2 - t1 + t3 - t4)/2 
▪ The implicit assumption in the above is that the one-way delay is statistically half 
the roundtrip delay and that the intrinsic drift rates of both the client and server 
clocks are small and  close to the same value.”
▪ The roundtrip delay d and clock offset c can then be used to estimate the 
correction to the applied to the local clock
▪ NTP does not specify how the local clock should be corrected
